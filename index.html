Section 1.1: The Enabling Act of 1889 & Constitutional Ratification

On February 22, 1889, the United States Congress passed the Enabling Act of 1889 (25 Stat. 676), authorizing the territories of Washington, Montana, North Dakota, and South Dakota to draft state constitutions and form state governments. Seventy-five delegates convened at the territorial capitol in Olympia on July 4, 1889, laboring for fifty-one days to draft a governing document reflecting late-nineteenth-century Populist and Progressive skepticism toward corporate monopolies and concentrated executive power.

Unlike the U.S. Constitution, which functions as a grant of enumerated powers to the federal government, the Washington State Constitution operates as a limitation on plenary state power. Because the state legislature possesses inherent authority to enact statutes unless explicitly restricted, the 1889 framers embedded strict constitutional mandates regarding public finance, common schools, and municipal debt ceilings.

Primary Source Excerpt — Article I, Section 1 (Political Power):
"All political power is inherent in the people, and governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed, and are established to protect and maintain individual rights."
Primary Source Excerpt — Article I, Section 7 (Invasion of Private Affairs):
"No person shall be disturbed in his private affairs, or his home invaded, without authority of law."
Judicial Note (State v. Gunwall, 1986): The Washington Supreme Court established six non-exclusive neutral criteria demonstrating that Article I, Section 7 affords broader protection against warrantless searches than the Fourth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution.

Section 1.2: Comparative Analysis of Federal and State Branches

Constitutional Provision U.S. Constitution (1787) Washington State Constitution (1889)
Executive Branch Structure Unitary Executive: President appoints Cabinet heads with Senate advice and consent (Art. II, Sec. 2). Plural Executive (Art. III, Sec. 1): Nine independently elected officials (Governor, Lt. Governor, Secretary of State, Treasurer, Auditor, Attorney General, Superintendent of Public Instruction, Commissioner of Public Lands, Insurance Commissioner).
Gubernatorial Veto Power Package Veto Only: President must sign or veto the entire enrolled bill (Art. I, Sec. 7). Partial / Line-Item Veto (Art. III, Sec. 12): Governor may veto individual sections of non-appropriation bills or specific appropriation items, subject to a two-thirds legislative override.
Citizen Direct Legislation None: All federal legislative authority is vested solely in Congress (Art. I, Sec. 1). Amendment 7 (1912) under Art. II, Sec. 1 reserves the power of the Initiative (8% signature threshold) and Referendum (4% signature threshold) to registered voters.
Public Education Mandate Not explicitly enumerated; reserved to states under the Tenth Amendment. Art. IX, Sec. 1 establishes the "paramount duty of the state" to amply fund K–12 basic education, enforced via McCleary v. State of Washington, 173 Wn.2d 477 (2012).

Section 1.3: The Bicameral Legislature & Statutory Process

Pursuant to Article II, the Washington State Legislature comprises a Senate (49 members serving four-year terms) and a House of Representatives (98 members serving two-year terms). Each of the state's 49 legislative districts—redrawn every ten years by a bipartisan five-member Redistricting Commission—elects one Senator and two Representatives (Position 1 and Position 2).

Section 2.1: Tribal Sovereignty & The Stevens Treaties (1854–1856)

In accordance with RCW 28A.320.170 (the Since Time Immemorial: Tribal Sovereignty in Washington State curriculum), students examine the legal and historical continuity of the twenty-nine federally recognized Indian tribes within Washington.

Chronology Treaty / Statutory Milestone Signatories & Legal Impact
Dec 26, 1854 Treaty of Medicine Creek (10 Stat. 1132) Negotiated at McAllister Creek with Nisqually, Puyallup, Steilacoom, and Squaxin Island leaders. Reserved the right of taking fish at all "usual and accustomed grounds and stations."
Jan 22, 1855 Treaty of Point Elliott (12 Stat. 927) Signed at Mukilteo (Point Elliott) by Chief Si'ahl (Suquamish/Duwamish), Chief Patkanim (Snoqualmie/Snohomish), Chow-its-hoot (Lummi), and Goliah (Skagit).
Jan 26, 1855 Treaty of Point No Point (12 Stat. 933) Signed at Hahd-skus on the Kitsap Peninsula with S'Klallam, Skokomish, and Chimakum bands.
Jan 31, 1855 Treaty of Neah Bay (12 Stat. 939) Negotiated with the Makah Nation at Cape Flattery, explicitly reserving marine whaling and sealing rights.
Feb 12, 1974 United States v. Washington, 384 F. Supp. 312 U.S. District Judge George H. Boldt ruled that "in common with" guarantees Treaty Tribes 50% of the harvestable salmon and steelhead returning to usual and accustomed grounds.

Section 3.1: County Charters, Municipal Incorporations & Special Districts

Local administration in Washington operates under Article XI of the State Constitution. While thirty-two counties operate under a standard three-member Board of County Commissioners, seven counties—King (1969), Clallam (1976), Whatcom (1978), Snohomish (1980), Pierce (1981), San Juan (2005), and Clark (2014)—have adopted Home Rule Charters under Article XI, Section 4.

Section 3.2: Washington Judicial Hierarchy (Article IV)

Court Tier Jurisdiction & Composition Statutory Authority
Washington Supreme Court Court of last resort in Olympia; 9 Justices elected statewide to 6-year terms. Art. IV, Sec. 2–4; Chapter 2.04 RCW
Court of Appeals 22 judges across three divisions: Division I (Seattle), Division II (Tacoma), and Division III (Spokane). Art. IV, Sec. 30; Chapter 2.06 RCW
Superior Courts Courts of general jurisdiction in all 39 counties; felony trials, civil suits, juvenile, and probate matters. Art. IV, Sec. 5–6; Chapter 2.08 RCW
District & Municipal Courts Courts of limited jurisdiction handling misdemeanors, traffic infractions, and municipal codes. Chapters 3.30 & 3.50 RCW

Section 1.1: The 1889 Constitutional Convention & The Failed 1878 Walla Walla Precedent

The path to Washington statehood required overcoming significant regional and legislative hurdles. Prior to the successful convention held in Olympia from July 4 to August 22, 1889, territorial delegates gathered in Walla Walla in 1878 to draft an earlier constitution. Although approved by Washington territorial voters, Congress rejected the 1878 document. However, the Walla Walla text served as a foundational blueprint eleven years later. When President Benjamin Harrison signed Proclamation 294 on November 11, 1889, Washington officially entered the Union as the 42nd state.

The 75 framers who assembled in Olympia incorporated robust populist reforms, ensuring that the Washington State Constitution would act as a direct restriction on plenary legislative power while establishing strict mandates for public education funding (Article IX) and rigorous privacy protections (Article I, Section 7).

Primary Source Excerpt — Article I, Section 7 (Invasion of Private Affairs):
"No person shall be disturbed in his private affairs, or his home invaded, without authority of law."
Expanded Legal Context: Interpreted via State v. Gunwall (1986), Washington courts recognize independent state constitutional grounds that provide broader safeguards against warrantless electronic and physical searches than the federal Fourth Amendment.

Section 1.2: The Plural Executive & Branch Separation

Unlike the federal model where the President appoints executive department heads, Article III of the Washington Constitution established a Plural Executive. Voters independently elect nine statewide executive officers, preventing the concentration of administrative power in a single office. Furthermore, Article III, Section 12 grants the Governor powerful line-item veto authority over non-appropriation bills and individual budget line items.

Section 2.1: Indigenous Sovereignty & The Stevens Treaties (1854–1856)

Enforced under RCW 28A.320.170 (Since Time Immemorial curriculum), Pacific Northwest history recognizes that sovereign Indigenous nations exercised jurisdiction across Puget Sound, the Olympic Peninsula, and the Columbia Plateau since time immemorial. Between 1854 and 1856, Governor Isaac Stevens negotiated treaties extinguishing territorial land titles while reserving permanent sovereign homelands and off-shore fishing rights.

Treaty / Milestone Date Signed Key Tribes & Legal Scope
Treaty of Medicine Creek Dec 26, 1854 Nisqually, Puyallup, Squaxin Island. Guaranteed usual and accustomed (U&A) fishing and gathering rights.
Treaty of Point Elliott Jan 22, 1855 Duwamish, Suquamish, Snoqualmie, Snohomish, Lummi, Skagit (signed by Chief Si'ahl at Mukilteo).
Treaty of Neah Bay Jan 31, 1855 Makah Nation. Reserved traditional deep-sea whaling and sealing rights in the Pacific Ocean.
Walla Walla Council June 9, 1855 Yakama Nation, Nez Perce, Umatilla, Cayuse. Established eastern plateau reservation boundaries.
The Boldt Decision Feb 12, 1974 U.S. v. Washington reaffirmed that "in common with" entitles Treaty Tribes to 50% of harvestable salmon.

Section 3.1: Home Rule Charters, Municipalities & Judicial Structure

Washington is organized into 39 counties and 281 incorporated cities. Under Article XI, Section 4 of the state constitution, counties may adopt voter-approved Home Rule Charters to replace traditional three-member commissioner boards with an elected County Council and Executive (King, Snohomish, Pierce, Whatcom, Clark, San Juan, and Clallam).

Section 3.2: Four-Tier Washington Court System (Article IV)

The state judiciary is structured hierarchically:

  • Supreme Court: 9 Justices elected to 6-year terms; state court of last resort.
  • Court of Appeals: 22 judges across 3 geographic divisions (Seattle, Tacoma, Spokane).
  • Superior Courts: General jurisdiction trial courts in all 39 counties.
  • District & Municipal Courts: Limited jurisdiction over misdemeanors and infractions.
